Educational Disparities Across India

Analyzing UDISE+ and ASER Survey Data to Uncover Inequalities in Access and Outcomes

Project Overview

This project examines educational disparities across India using data from UDISE+ (Unified District Information System for Education Plus) and other sources. Through a series of data visualizations, we explore patterns of inequality across geographic, gender, and socioeconomic dimensions to understand the challenges facing India's education system.

Key Questions: How do enrollment rates vary across Indian states? What gender disparities exist in education access? How do rural and urban areas compare in educational outcomes?

Data

The data used for this project comes from 3 sources:

1) UDISE+ (Unified District Information System for Education Plus): This comprehensive database provides school-level information across India, including enrollment statistics by gender and location, infrastructure metrics (electricity, internet, libraries, toilets), and student-teacher ratios. The data covers the period 2021-2024 and represents nearly all schools in India.

2) ASER (Annual Status of Education Report) 2024: Conducted by Pratham, ASER is India's largest citizen-led survey measuring children's learning outcomes. It assesses basic reading (ability to read Standard II level text) and arithmetic (ability to perform division) skills across Standards III, V, and VIII in rural India.

3) Census of India: Population and demographic data used to contextualize enrollment figures and calculate rates.

Analytical Challenges

The biggest challenges in this project were the following:

1) Data integration across multiple sources: Combining UDISE+ enrollment data with ASER learning outcomes required careful alignment of state names, time periods, and measurement units. Some states had missing data for certain years, requiring interpolation or exclusion from specific analyses.

2) Creating meaningful composite indices: The infrastructure quality index required normalizing and weighting four different metrics (electricity, internet, libraries, toilets) to create a single comparable measure across states. Determining appropriate weights and handling missing facilities data presented methodological challenges.

3) Visualization of multidimensional relationships: Displaying relationships between infrastructure, learning outcomes, gender, location, and time required careful selection of visualization types. I used choropleth maps for geographic patterns, heatmaps for comparing learning outcomes across states and standards, scatter plots for correlations, and network diagrams for state similarity clustering.

4) Clustering states into meaningful archetypes: Using hierarchical clustering on five education metrics required normalizing different scales (percentages, ratios, index scores) and determining the optimal number of clusters that revealed genuinely distinct state profiles rather than arbitrary divisions.

I. Access & Enrollment Disparities

Gender Gap in School Enrollment

This diverging bar chart reveals significant gender disparities in school enrollment across Indian states. The horizontal bars show the percentage point difference between girls' and boys' enrollment rates. States like Haryana and Punjab show substantial gaps favoring boys (green bars extending left), indicating fewer girls enrolled relative to boys. In contrast, southern states like Kerala and Tamil Nadu approach gender parity or show slight advantages for girls (purple bars extending right).

Key Finding: Northern states exhibit the most pronounced gender gaps (up to 7 percentage points), while southern and northeastern states demonstrate greater gender parity in education access. The gender gap pronounces to be reducing over 2022-2024.

Educational Progression and Dropout Patterns

This visualization examines enrollment patterns by gender across different states, revealing where students are concentrated in the education system and how gender ratios vary by region.

Key Finding: Enrollment drops dramatically between Class 10 and Class 11 nationwide, and across individual states. The patterns remain similar for both women and men. Use the state dropdown to explore regional differences in dropout patterns.

School Type Distribution: Rural vs Urban Divide

This area chart reveals stark differences in school type distribution between rural and urban areas across India.

Key Finding: Rural areas remain heavily dominated by state government schools, while urban areas show increasing privatization - private unaided schools grew for urban enrollment between 2021-2024. Rural private school enrollment has remained stable.

II. Educational Inputs: School Infrastructure

This choropleth map visualizes the composite infrastructure index across Indian states for 2022-2024. The index combines four key metrics: electricity availability, internet connectivity, library access, and functional girls' toilets.

Key Finding: A clear geographic pattern emerges, with southern states (Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Telangana) and western states (Gujarat, Maharashtra) showing infrastructure scores above 80%, while several northeastern states lag behind with scores below 50%. This geographic pattern suggests systematic regional disparities in educational investment and development.

III. Learning Outcomes: Measuring Educational Success

This dual heatmap displays reading and math proficiency across Standards III, V, and VIII for Indian states, using ASER 2024 data.

Key Finding: A clear performance hierarchy emerges. Northern states (Himachal Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana) and northeastern states (Mizoram, Nagaland) achieve the highest outcomes (80-90% reading at Std VIII), while southern states surprisingly lag despite superior infrastructure. Mathematics proficiency is significantly lower than reading across all states — most show fewer than 40% of Std VIII students able to perform division.

IV. What Drives Educational Success?

Infrastructure & Class Size: Do They Matter for Learning?

We examine whether better school infrastructure and lower student-teacher ratios correlate with improved learning outcomes across Indian states.

Key Finding: Neither infrastructure quality nor student-teacher ratios show significant correlation with learning outcomes (all r < 0.22, p > 0.28). States with the highest infrastructure scores do not necessarily achieve the best results, pointing to other factors—such as teacher quality, curriculum effectiveness, and socioeconomic conditions—as more critical determinants of educational success.

State Similarity Network: Uncovering Education System Archetypes

This network uses hierarchical clustering on five education metrics—reading proficiency, math proficiency, infrastructure quality, student-teacher ratio, and rural school percentage—to identify states with similar education profiles. Edge thickness represents similarity strength, with connections appearing only when states exceed 70% similarity across all dimensions.

Key Finding: The clustering reveals a striking infrastructure paradox. Southern and western states (Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Telangana) form a distinct cluster with India's best school infrastructure yet achieve the poorest reading outcomes. Meanwhile, states like Himachal Pradesh and Mizoram cluster together based on educational success despite moderate resources, while Bihar, Jharkhand, and Uttar Pradesh outperform well-resourced states in mathematics despite extreme overcrowding (high student-teacher ratio). This suggests that infrastructure quality and class size are less important than pedagogy and teaching effectiveness.

Final Thoughts

This project reveals that educational disparities across India are driven more by teaching quality and pedagogical effectiveness than by infrastructure or class size alone. While infrastructure gaps remain important, particularly in northeastern states, the data demonstrates that resource-rich states are failing to convert investments into learning outcomes.

The emergence of distinct state archetypes—from "Infrastructure-Rich, Outcome-Poor" southern states to "Best-in-Class Performers" in the north and northeast—indicates that one-size-fits-all policies will not address India's education challenges. States like Kerala and Tamil Nadu possess excellent infrastructure yet struggle with learning outcomes, while states like Himachal Pradesh and Mizoram achieve excellence with moderate resources. This suggests that classroom pedagogy, teacher training, and curriculum effectiveness matter far more than facilities.

The analysis also highlights critical challenges that require immediate attention: the dramatic dropout crisis at the Class 10-11 transition, persistent gender gaps of up to 7 percentage points in northern states, and a nationwide mathematics proficiency crisis where fewer than 40% of Standard VIII students can perform basic division.

Addressing these disparities requires moving beyond traditional metrics of educational investment to focus on what happens inside classrooms. Policymakers should prioritize teacher quality, curriculum innovation, and pedagogical training while simultaneously tackling gender barriers and the secondary education transition crisis. States should learn from high-performing regions and adapt strategies based on their specific archetype rather than pursuing generic reforms.
